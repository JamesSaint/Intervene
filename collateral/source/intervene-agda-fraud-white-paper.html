IThe question you will be asked

Fraud now has a clock and a price. Since 7 October 2024, a firm that releases an authorised push payment (APP) fraud can owe the victim up to £85,000, with the cost shared between the sending and receiving firm. Since 30 October 2024, a firm may hold a suspect outbound payment for up to four business days.

Firms can show that their alerts are good. What they rarely record is how long it takes, on one type of payment, for an alert to reach someone who can hold the payment, for that person to decide, and for the hold to work.

A supervisor, or a reimbursement dispute, will ask one question: could this payment have been held in time? A first step is an Authority Check on one type of payment. We find out who can hold a payment, how quickly they can be reached, and whether the hold works. Then we test it with one real call outside office hours.

This paper is for you if
  • You send or receive push payments at volume.
  • You answer for fraud losses, reimbursement or the controls behind them.
  • You must be able to show why a flagged payment left.
  • You want fewer losses without holding up good customers.
Payments in scope
  • Outbound Faster Payments: where most APP scams leave.
  • High-value and CHAPS payments: fewer, larger, slower to recover.
  • Inbound payments: restricting a suspected mule account.
  • Automated fraud decisions: holds, releases and extra checks.

IIThe gap: the time you have, and the time you take

A fraud policy names who may hold a payment. It rarely records how long reaching that person takes at the weekend, whether they can decide alone, or whether the hold is set in the system that releases the payment. Those are the delays that decide whether the money leaves.

Together these set the time available. Before a payment is sent, it may be seconds. Where the power to delay applies, it can stretch to four business days. After release, recovery depends on the receiving firm acting before the money moves on. In each case, the firm either acts in time or pays.

IIIWhat it costs when action comes late

Stopping a fraudulent payment takes four steps: detect, escalate, decide and hold. If one takes too long, the others cannot make up the time. Each case below shows one step failing. Steps further along fail more quietly: they show up as losses and reimbursement claims, not as fines.

A firm can fix detection and escalation and still lose the money if the approver is away or the hold is set in the wrong system. The worked example on page 3 shows how those later steps break.

IVWhat your assurance shows, and what it leaves open

Fraud model testing shows whether alerts are good. Control testing shows that procedures exist and are followed. Loss and claims data show what it cost. The gap is time: how long it takes, at the weekend, with the usual approver away, to get from an alert to a hold that works. The rules you answer to leave that open too.

VHow to evidence it

AGDA® FRAUD applies Intervene's AGDA® assessment to one type of payment and one fraud scenario. It asks four questions.

1DetectDoes anyone see the suspect payment before it goes?
2EscalateDoes the alert reach someone who can hold it, in time?
3DecideCan they decide to hold it, alone, at 2am?
4InterveneDoes the hold stop the payment before it leaves?
Evidence weighed fromAssumedPolicyDrillProductionStress-tested

We grade each step by the best evidence behind it, and time it. We add the times and compare the total with the time available: how long before the loss is more than the firm can accept or recover. We work that out from the firm's own records, such as payment volumes at that hour, its fraud risk appetite, and when the money is gone, and show how the verdict changes if the time is 25% shorter or longer.

Fraud model testing shows the alert is good. This shows whether anyone acts on it in time. AGDA® FRAUD does not detect fraud or block payments, and your controls stay as they are. Intervene has no vendor affiliation and does not implement what it recommends.

Worked example
Constructed example · No real organisation · Not a client result

A building society's outbound Faster Payments, Sunday 22:30. A new investment scam sends members' savings to first-time payees. Each payment scores just below the level that would hold it automatically, so it goes out. The society's appetite tolerates no more than £150,000 of reimbursable loss per incident. Losses build at about £30,000 an hour. Time available: about five hours.

2 EscalateThe analyst cannot change the hold level. The on-call fraud manager is paged.45 min2 hDrill: two call-out tests
3 DecideLowering the hold level will also stop genuine payments. Policy says the Head of Fraud must agree, and is silent on who decides out of hours.30 min3 h 30 minPolicy only: never tested

End to endAgainst about five hours available2 h 15 min9 h

Verdict: At risk. At best, the payments are held well in time. At worst, they are held four hours late, after around £270,000 has left. With 25% more or less time, the verdict is the same.

Weak link: Decide. The rule to hold suspect payments exists. Who may tighten it at night does not. That one gap can use most of the time available.

Fix first: allow the on-call fraud manager to lower the hold level for this kind of scam for 24 hours; agree the Consumer Duty test for doing so in advance; test it by drill.

Before impactThe payment can be held in time, under the conditions tested.
At riskIt can be held, but with little time to spare.
After impactIt cannot be held in time. The money leaves.

VIIResearch foundations and validation status

The intervention assurance methodology draws on research into risk velocity, intervention latency and the conditions under which effective control can be demonstrated. SEDI provides the conceptual structure for examining the intervention sequence; AGDA® FRAUD applies that structure to one type of payment and one fraud scenario.

The research programme includes theoretical development and retrospective analysis. Prospective empirical evaluation will examine assessment findings against observed intervention performance, including findings that challenge the methodology. Commercial pilot assessments may contribute to this programme where research participation and data use have been separately agreed, subject to confidentiality and research integrity requirements.

AGDA® FRAUD is in research and development. Its fraud-specific components, including an uncalibrated probabilistic estimate of whether intervention completes before a payment's deadline, are tested on synthetic data only, not client data. Neither it nor AGDA® is validated against real-world outcomes.

Assessment verdicts are specific to their scope, evidence and assumptions. Neither academic publication nor successful verification of an assessment artefact constitutes independent validation of the commercial instrument or a guarantee of future control effectiveness.
